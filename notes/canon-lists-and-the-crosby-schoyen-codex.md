# The Crosby-Schøyen Codex, Melito's canon list, and what this repository can host

Research note for the Patristica entry writers. Nothing here is a hosted text.
It exists because material about the codex was supplied to this repository and
almost none of it can be hosted — but the underlying facts can be used, and one
of the primary sources is already in the repository.

## The short answer to "is this the earliest known canon?"

Two different things get run together under that phrase.

**The Crosby-Schøyen Codex is not a canon list.** It is a book — a Coptic
papyrus codex containing several works bound together. It is evidence about how
early Christians collected and used texts, and it carries the earliest complete
surviving copies of two biblical books, but it does not say which books are
scripture and it was never intended to.

**The earliest surviving Christian list of Old Testament books is Melito of
Sardis', and this repository already carries it.** Melito, bishop of Sardis,
wrote to a man named Onesimus with a list of "the books of the Old Testament,"
and Eusebius quotes the letter in full. It survives at
`church-fathers/eusebius-church-history-books-4-10.txt`, Book IV, chapter 26 —
search for "Onesimus". Two things about it are usually noted: it is the earliest
known Christian use of the phrase "Old Testament" for a body of writings, and
the list omits Esther. The earliest surviving list of *New Testament* books is
the Muratorian fragment, already at `church-fathers/muratorian-canon.txt`.

Both of those are in the repository, verbatim, in public-domain translations.
An entry on early canon lists can quote them directly.

## What the Crosby-Schøyen Codex actually is

A single-quire papyrus codex written in Sahidic Coptic. Its contents, in order:

- Melito of Sardis, *Peri Pascha* (*On the Passover*), chapters 47–105
- 2 Maccabees 5:27–7:41, headed "The Jewish Martyrs"
- 1 Peter, headed "The Letter of Peter"
- Jonah
- an unidentified concluding text, fragmentary, possibly a hymn

It belongs to the hoard usually called the Bodmer Papyri, found near Dishna in
Upper Egypt in 1952 and associated with the library of the Pachomian monasteries.
The main body of the codex is MS 193 in the Schøyen Collection; further leaves
are in the Chester Beatty Library in Dublin.

**Dating is genuinely contested.** Published estimates have ranged across the
second to sixth centuries. The Schøyen Collection's own catalogue assigns it to
the third century. Radiocarbon analysis of a sample was published by Hugo
Lundhaug in the *Bulletin of the American Society of Papyrologists* 57 (2020),
219–234; that article discusses at length how much such a result can and cannot
settle. An entry should say the date is disputed rather than pick a number.

Treat the superlatives with care. Claims that it is "the oldest Christian
liturgical manuscript" or "the oldest book in private ownership" come from the
collection's own catalogue description of a manuscript it owns, and one of those
claims is about the antiquities market rather than about Christian history. The
defensible statements are narrower: it carries the earliest known complete text
of 1 Peter and of Jonah, and it is among the earliest surviving examples of
several works gathered into one codex.

## Why almost none of this can be hosted

- **Melito, *On Pascha*.** The full text was recovered only in the twentieth
  century, from this hoard and from the Chester Beatty and Michigan papyri. Every
  English translation of it is therefore modern and in copyright. The copy
  supplied to this project is Alistair Stewart-Sykes' translation, St Vladimir's
  Seminary Press, Popular Patristics Series 20 — in copyright, not hostable.
  What the repository can carry is what the 1885 Ante-Nicene Fathers carried:
  the fragments, already at `church-fathers/melito-fragments.txt`.
- **The scholarly articles.** Lundhaug (2020) and Pietersma and Comstock, *BASP*
  48 (2011) 27–46, are current academic publications. Their findings are facts
  and may be reported and cited; their text may not be copied.
- **The Schøyen Collection's catalogue page and Brent Nongbri's blog.** Both are
  the writing of their authors. Facts yes, wording no.
- **The Wesley Huff study material.** A living author's own writing and speech.
  It can be a pointer to a topic. It cannot be a source of text, and where it
  compresses "Melito's canon list" and "the Crosby-Schøyen Codex" into one claim,
  the entry should separate them as above.

## Suggested shape for the entry

Lead with what a reader can actually read for themselves: Melito's list in
Eusebius and the Muratorian fragment, both one tap away in this app. Then use
the codex as the physical illustration — a real book, from a real monastery
library, that shows several writings already bound together, with a date
scholars still argue about. That way the entry rests on primary sources the app
holds, and the manuscript adds the picture rather than carrying the argument.
